Water Heater Leak Cleanup in Shoreline: What to Know

The tank on a water heater stores 40 to 80 gallons, yet a leak can release far more than that. Your supply line keeps feeding the tank, which means it fills back up just as quickly as water escapes through a rusted bottom. It will run until the cold water valve gets closed. The location of the heater sets the damage. It may be a hallway closet, a garage floor or, the worst case, an attic over finished rooms.

In Shoreline, we start with water extraction and then inspect everything around the heater. Usually the tank platform and the lower part of the closet walls have taken on the most water. In a garage, water reaches the wall shared with the house and anything stored on the floor. From an attic it comes down through insulation and ceilings. Each of those areas is metered, then dried using dehumidifiers and air movers.

Do This First

Your First Steps

  1. Cut the Power or GasFor an electric heater, switch off its breaker. With a gas heater, the knob on the control goes to the off position. A burner or heating element must not stay on once the tank is empty.
  2. Shut the Cold Water ValveThe cold line over the tank has a valve, and it closes with a clockwise turn. If it is stuck or leaking, shut off the main water valve for the house.
  3. Watch for Hot WaterWhat comes out of the tank can cause scalds. Pets and children should stay clear, and nobody should walk into pooled water before it cools down.
  4. Empty the Tank OutsideA garden hose on the drain valve, if you can get to it safely, lets you send what is left in the tank outside or into a floor drain instead of across the floor.
  5. Move Stored ItemsAnything absorbent, including boxes and rugs, should come up from the wet area. Cardboard and fabric soak up water quickly and hold it against walls and concrete.

Insurance

Does Insurance Cover It?

When a tank fails suddenly, the water damage to floors, walls and belongings is often covered as sudden and accidental. Replacing the heater is usually not included, because a tank wearing out from age counts as maintenance. Because coverage usually does not extend to gradual leaks, a claim may be denied or reduced when the adjuster sees indications the tank leaked for a long time, like old stains or trails of rust. Read your policy, take photos of the damage and the heater, and do not dispose of the old unit before the claim is settled.

What It Costs

What Affects the Cost

  • Placement of the unit, as leaks from an attic or an inside closet run higher than those in a garage.
  • Condition of the stand or subfloor below the heater, which may need rebuilding before the replacement is set.
  • The amount of wall drywall, ceiling drywall or insulation that has to be removed.
  • If water got to the flooring of adjoining rooms, and the kind of flooring involved.

Why It Happens

What Causes It

  • The drain valve at the bottom of the tank cracking, loosening or failing to shut fully.
  • Anode rods that are used up, leaving the steel inside the tank open to rust.
  • An aging steel tank whose bottom is eaten through by corrosion and sediment.
  • Flex connectors or inlet and outlet fittings at the top of the heater that have corroded.
  • The relief valve for temperature and pressure opening, or failing, and letting water out.
What Is at Risk

Where the Damage Spreads

Platform and Subfloor

The wooden stand or subfloor under the tank takes the most water. It can rot and weaken, which matters when a new full tank is set on it.

Heater Closet Drywall

Water wicks up the drywall around an interior heater closet and into the rooms on the other side. Baseboards swell and the lower wall stays wet inside.

Garage Wall Base

Water crossing a garage slab soaks the bottom plate and drywall of the wall shared with the house, then moves into the flooring on the living side.

Attic and Ceilings

When an attic heater leaks, water passes through the insulation and lands on the ceiling underneath. Wet insulation stops insulating and its weight can bring ceiling drywall down.
